Pacing optional — add suggested min/mi paces alongside effort

Enter a recent race result, the paces you already know, or both. Leave everything blank for effort-only guidance.

Paces come from the Daniels/Gilbert race-equivalency formulas, which estimate equal-effort paces from one race result. Easy covers roughly 62–70% of your aerobic capacity, tempo about 88%, and the race-effort range adds a small buffer (15 s/mi for the marathon, 6 s/mi for the half) to the predicted pace. These formulas tend to be optimistic, especially for a first marathon, so treat paces as a ceiling and let effort decide.

What the terms mean: Strides
Short, relaxed accelerations of 15–20 seconds at the end of an easy run, with a full walk or jog recovery between them. Quick and smooth, never a sprint. They're optional; skip them if they're new to you or anything hurts.
Tempo
"Comfortably hard," about 6–7 out of 10. You can say a few words but not hold a conversation. Done as intervals inside an easy run, with easy running before and after.
Race effort
The effort you could sustain for your whole goal race: steady and controlled. Marathon effort is a little quicker than easy; half-marathon effort sits closer to tempo. It's rehearsed in short segments during the race-specific phase.
Cutback
A planned lighter week, about 25% less distance, that lets your body adapt. It doesn't mean you're behind, so don't add miles back in.
Readiness gate
The dark bars between phases list what should be true before you move on. If you're not there yet, repeat a week or two and shift the rest of the plan back. Weeks are numbered, not dated, for exactly this reason.

About suggested paces: a recent race result is useful evidence of fitness, but equivalency formulas tend to be optimistic for the marathon, especially for first-timers or runners under about 40 mi (65 km) per week. Start marathon-effort work at the slower end of the range, and never turn easy days into pace tests.

Readiness gates (the calendar assumes you pass these)

  • Into Build: several consistent weeks, normal sleep/appetite/mood, no persistent pain, long runs recovered within ~24–48 h.
  • Into the race-specific phase: for a marathon, 30–35 mi (48–56 km) per week feels sustainable and 14–16 mi (23–26 km) long runs are comfortable; for a half, 20–25 mi (32–40 km) and 8–10 mi (13–16 km). No recurring pain or lingering fatigue.
  • If you don't pass: repeat the previous week (or block) and slide the remaining weeks back. There's no penalty for repeating.
  • Peak long run (marathon 16–20 mi / 26–32 km; half 10–13 mi / 16–21 km) is a ceiling, not a requirement. Don't attempt the upper end if shorter peaks leave you depleted or sore.

Nutrition

  • No fixed calorie target without age, sex, height, weight, health context, and activity. Eat enough for daily needs plus training; avoid intentional restriction while building endurance unless supervised.
  • Running energy: roughly 1 kcal per kg per km (≈0.7 kcal per lb per mile). Add it to usual needs — it's not a ceiling.
  • Carbohydrate: about 5–7 g/kg/day in moderate training, more on long or demanding days as tolerated. Protein: about 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day, spread across meals.
  • Before long runs: familiar carb-rich meal or snack 1–4 h before. During runs over 75–90 min: practice 30–60 g carbs/hour; build toward more only gradually. After: carbs + protein + fluids.
  • Low-energy warning signs: persistent fatigue, declining performance, irritability, frequent illness, poor sleep, recurrent injury, cold sensitivity, menstrual changes, reduced libido. Reduce load and consult a clinician or sports dietitian.

  • Pair strength with easy runs; never hard lower-body work the day before the long run. One consistent session beats two that hurt your running.
  • Muscle effort is expected; sharp, joint, or worsening pain, numbness, or a gait change is not — stop and get advice.

Hydration

BeforeStart normally hydrated; drink with meals and to thirst. Don't force large volumes.
Long runs / heatCarry or plan fluids; consider electrolytes for long, sweaty, or hot sessions. Estimate sweat rate from pre/post body mass, accounting for fluid taken in.
AfterDrink to thirst with food; after heavy sweating replace fluid and sodium with meals rather than chugging.
AvoidGaining weight during exercise from drinking — overhydration can cause dangerous hyponatremia.

Possible heat illness: unusual weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache, chills in heat, or worsening coordination — stop, cool down, get help as needed. Confusion, collapse, seizure, or altered mental status is an emergency: call emergency services and start rapid cooling.

When to modify, stop, or seek care

  • Modify or rest: poor sleep, illness, heavy legs that don't improve in warm-up, soreness that changes your stride, fatigue lasting more than 1–2 days.
  • Stop and get assessed: localized bone pain, pain that worsens as you run, swelling, limping, pain at rest or night, recurring symptoms.
  • Urgent care: chest pressure/pain, fainting, severe or unusual shortness of breath, palpitations with dizziness, confusion, collapse.
  • Check with a clinician first if you have cardiovascular, metabolic, kidney, or other significant conditions, take medicines affecting heart rate or fluid balance, are pregnant/postpartum, or have a history of eating disorder or stress fractures.

Weekly self-check

  • □ I slept reasonably well.
  • □ Appetite and energy feel normal.
  • □ No pain is changing my stride.
  • □ Easy pace feels easy.
  • □ I fueled longer sessions.
  • □ I drank to thirst and considered conditions.
  • □ I had at least one rest day.
  • □ I didn't make up missed runs.
  • □ I could repeat this week without dread.
  • □ I'm willing to hold or reduce load if needed.

Decision rule: several "no" answers → repeat or reduce the week. Persistent or worsening symptoms → stop and seek professional advice.
